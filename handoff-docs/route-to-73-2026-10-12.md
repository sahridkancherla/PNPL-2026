# Route to 73: Revised for Open Holdout Use


## What changed

The organisers have confirmed that the team may use the holdout data in any way before generating a submission. That removes the main limit the 11 Oct report worked under, so the ranking changes.

**What it opens up**

- **Training on holdout MEG:** A2 (members copying the mix on the holdout), self-training on pseudo-labels, BatchNorm adaptation.
- **Transductive inference:** treating the 868 sentence rows as one set, so rows of the same word can be grouped and each word's share of the holdout estimated.
- **Statistics of the holdout recording itself.** Holdout-own scaling is already tested: trial 15 found it made no difference.
- **Possibly, other people's recordings of the same sentences.** pnpl releases holdout files for all 40 subjects (subj00 to subj39). If they heard the sentences subject 0 heard, each row has up to 39 more recordings of the same word.

**What it does not change**

- Rule 4 still discourages inferring labels "outside of the official evaluation procedure". So no probing the board for labels, and no outside lookup of the sentence list.
- Top teams still hand over runnable checkpoints. Any step that adapts on the holdout must sit inside the pipeline, seeded and re-runnable.
- Results already on the board stand.
- One question is worth an email: the Deep track is defined on subject 0, so confirm that "the holdout data" includes subjects 1 to 39.

## Bottom line

Three changes that use the holdout itself now lead the list. Other subjects' recordings of the same sentences could be worth more than everything else combined, or nothing; one quick check decides. Transductive inference and self-training on holdout MEG are safer, but each is worth only a point or two, so they must stack to reach 73.

## Diagnosis

The mix is stuck because 20 of its 23 files share one view of the data, so the rows they all miss stay missed. Nine recent files that re-weighted, re-seeded or tweaked members all landed between 68.5 and 69.2.

The missing rows are most likely in three places:

- **Rare words.** Every word counts equally, but the holdout's word mix is uneven: on 7 Oct a single flipped row moved the board 2.0 points, so at least one word has only one public row. Decoding is weakest for words that are rare in training ([d'Ascoli et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12658044/)), and each such word can swing the score by one or two points.
- **The holdout session.** It is a new recording with new stimuli. Per-channel scaling is not the problem (trial 15: no difference), but nothing so far has adapted the models to that recording.
- **Sentence edges.** First words follow silence and last words are followed by it. Most training items have neither.

One explanation in the 11 Oct report no longer holds. It blamed the sentence transformer for seeing only vocabulary words at test time, but trial 17's L trained on exactly such runs and lost 24 points. The fix is not more vocabulary-only training.

The one holdout-wide step tried so far, the hub correction, gained +0.9. It is a crude form of transductive inference, which suggests the holdout's own structure carries usable signal.

## Ranked changes

The order is by expected board gain; the chart above carries the estimates. Each change says what to build, why, a label-free first check, and cost and risk.

### 1. Other subjects' recordings of the same sentences

**Not the same as training on other subjects.** That was tried and lost (all-data G2, 54.79 alone). This change trains nothing new for subject 0. It takes other people's recordings of the same test sentences and adds them as extra votes on those rows, so every word is measured several times instead of once.

**First check, label-free and quick.** Load the sentence files for all 40 subjects. For each subject-0 sentence, look for sentences in other files with the same number of words and the same onsets to within one sample (4 ms at 250 Hz). Matching onsets mean the same audio was played. Check subject 0's own file for repeated sentences too. Lone words can't be matched this way.

**If they match, two ways to use them:**

- **Extra votes.** Decode each matched recording with a multi-subject model, such as the all-data G2 recipe (subject 0 plus the 12 published subjects, each with its own subject layer). Add the other subjects' z-scored scores to subject 0's rows at a low weight, then apply the usual corrections. Leave out subjects with no training data at first.
- **Alignment.** Fit a multiway CCA across subjects on the matched sentences. It finds the signal all listeners share and "can be applied effectively to multi-subject datasets" to denoise them ([de Cheveigné et al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/344960v1)). Map each other recording into subject 0's sensor space, then run subject 0's own models on it as extra votes.

**Why it could be large.** This is not the "more data" that cost 8.6 points in trial 18: it adds measurements of the test items themselves. d'Ascoli et al. found decoding "steadily increases with the number of decoding predictions used for averaging", including averaging "across different subjects for a given context", though more slowly than across contexts. The organisers' Broad-track references reach 33.13 (d'Ascoli) and 42.96 (MEG-XL) BAcc@10 on other subjects, against 20% chance ([competition paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.03231v1)).

**How much, roughly.** I ran a toy simulation in which every listener shares the same signal and the same stimulus-driven confusions, and weaker listeners add more private noise. Twelve extra listeners at 30% each lift a 70% decoder to about 77% if a quarter of its errors are shared confusions, 73 if half are, and 71 if three-quarters are. The catch: if the other subjects are weak because their signal is weaker rather than noisier, full-weight votes lower the score. Weight them low.

**Choosing the weight without labels.** The LibriBrain100 paper says subjects 1 to 32 heard only Sherlock (two chapters each) and lists validation and test hours for them ([LibriBrain100](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.25204)). If those are chapters subject 0 also heard, test the averaging there first, with labels. Otherwise weight each subject by its agreement with subject 0's mix, and spend two submissions on two weights.

**Cost and risk.** Minutes for the check, about a day for a multi-subject decoder. The main risk is the permission, so send the one confirmation question first.

### 2. Transductive inference over the holdout rows

**What to build.** For each member, take its embeddings of the 868 sentence rows and fit a mixture model over them, in the style of TransCLIP ([Zanella et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01837v1)):

- **One cluster per word,** started at that word's training prototype: the mean embedding of its training occurrences on holdout-shaped items. This is where the 11 Oct report's prototypes now go.
- **A pull toward the member's original scores** (TransCLIP's KL term), so clusters can't drift far from what the model saw.
- **A graph term** that gives similar rows similar scores, built from each row's nearest rows in other sentences only. Rows from the same sentence look alike because the transformer mixes context.
- **Word shares estimated from the data,** with a floor, not fixed equal. TransCLIP assumes balanced classes, and transductive methods tested on imbalanced sets show "substantial performance drops, even below inductive methods in some cases" ([Veilleux et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.11181v1)).

Blend the result with the original scores, z-score each member, average, and submit with and without the hub correction.

**Also: kNN memory scores.** Store each member's embeddings of every training occurrence of the 50 words, cut like holdout items. Score each holdout row by a vote among its nearest stored instances, and add that as an extra file. It needs no holdout statistics.

**Why.** The members are contrastive models that score test items against text-side word vectors, which is the zero-shot CLIP set-up. TransCLIP raised zero-shot CLIP by 5.0 points on average over 11 datasets and by 3.7 on ImageNet (ViT-B/16). ZLaP's label propagation gave +2.1 over 14 datasets ([Stojnić et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04072)).

**Label-free check.** Before: do rows with the same top-1 word sit closer to each other than to other rows, leaving out same-sentence pairs? After: seeds of a family should agree more, not less (A0's damage showed as less), and the estimated shares should look plausible, with common function words high.

**Cost and risk.** Hours, no training. The risk is rare words: with one or two rows they can't form clusters, and a strong pull toward big clusters buries them. The pull to the original scores and the floor on word shares guard against that.

### 3. Self-training on holdout MEG

**What to build.** First submit A2 (ens\_a2\_blend) as planned; it is now within the rules. Then a stronger round:

- **Teacher:** the best mix after change 2, plus change 1 if it works, so the targets beat the 69.76 mix.
- **Students:** members fine-tuned on the holdout rows to match the teacher's soft scores. Give them noise (the sensor-disc dropout they trained with) and mix in ordinary training batches so they don't forget.
- **Rows:** start with the rows where the teacher is most confident, then widen.
- **Use:** add students beside their parents, and also test them in place of parents.

**Why.** Noisy Student training reached 88.4% on ImageNet, 2.0 points above the previous best. The noise given to the student ("dropout, stochastic depth, and data augmentation") is what lets it beat its teacher ([Xie et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.04252)). Here the gain would come from adapting members to the holdout recording, which nothing has done yet.

**Label-free check.** Use the A2 signals: students should move toward an independent model (B), and their agreement with each other should stay below about 90%, so the mix keeps its different mistakes. A2's students agree 81 to 85%.

**Cost and risk.** About a day of GPU time per round. The risk is that students copy the teacher's errors and the mix loses diversity, which is why both placements get tested.

### 4. A better per-word correction

**What to build.** Variants of the hub correction, one file each:

- **Leave out each word's likely rows.** Today each word's correction is computed over all rows, including the ones that probably are that word, which over-corrects frequent words. Weight each row by one minus its probability of being that word.
- **Estimate the word shares, then correct for them.** Use maximum likelihood (EM) after bias-corrected calibration, which "outperforms" other label-shift fixes ([Alexandari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.06852)). Then balance with Sinkhorn to those estimated shares, never to equal ones.
- **By position:** a separate correction for first, middle and last words, shrunk toward the overall one.
- **The pending balance\_half file (hubb).**

* **Top-10 quota assignment.** Solve a transport problem where each row has 10 slots and each word gets slots in proportion to its estimated share, then take each row's top 10 from the plan. It aims at top-10 recall directly, not top-1.
* **QB-Norm with a bank of training items** (Bogolin et al. 2022). A hubness fix that uses no holdout statistics, worth one file as a second opinion on the hub correction.

**Why.** Every word counts equally in the score, so removing each word's bias pays. The current correction's +0.9 shows the bias is real.

**Label-free check.** Count how often each word enters a top 10. The spread should narrow without pushing frequent words far below their estimated share.

Before submitting, run each variant on local MOCHA, TIMIT and Moth fragments resampled to an uneven word mix, and drop any that lose there.

**Cost and risk.** Minutes per file. Differences will be small against a noisy board, so judge these variants as a group, not one by one.

### 5. Lone rows, for the private board

All 92 lone rows sit on the private board, which decides the final ranking. If they are spread evenly over the 50 words, about two each, they make up a large part of the private score for rare words, which have few sentence rows. Nothing on the public board can measure them.

**What to build.**

- **A lone-word mix.** Combine I3 (41.6 locally) with the continuous family's lone predictions (SW 39.0, SPW 38.3, B 38.3), weighted by the local lone-word test. That test has the right shape, unlike the fragment tests.
- **Adapt it to the holdout session.** Fine-tune I3 on 1 s windows from the holdout sentences, labelled by the best sentence mix (change 3's recipe), then re-predict the lone rows.

**Label-free check.** The lone members' agreement on the 92 rows, and whether the adapted I3 still agrees with the original on most of them.

**Cost and risk.** Hours for the mix, about a day for the adaptation. Sentence windows contain the next words and lone epochs don't, so keep the original I3 in the blend.

### 6. New voters from cues the members barely use

Kept from the 11 Oct report, with one change:

- **Onset gaps.** The gap to the next onset approximates a word's spoken length. Fit how gaps vary by word on training data, dividing by each sentence's median gap to remove speaking rate. Then re-fit it on the holdout by EM with the mix's soft labels, since the holdout's speech may differ from the Sherlock narrator's. Add it as a low-weight voter for every word except the last.
- **Phoneme matching.** Score each word by aligning its pronunciation to SPW's phoneme outputs between this onset and the next. Lower priority: trial 15 judged single-trial phoneme decoding too weak to build words from.

**Why.** Every past gain came from a member that sees the problem differently, and a pure timing voter is the most different view left.

**Label-free check.** Correlate the gap with the length of the mix's top-1 word. A weak link means the mix isn't using the cue.

**Cost and risk.** Hours for the gap voter. It is weak alone, so it only helps at a low weight.

### Lower priority

- **Session alignment.** Re-colour the holdout recording's sensor covariance to match training, as Euclidean alignment does for EEG ([He and Wu](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.05464)). Per-channel scaling made no difference, so expect little. Work in the signal subspace: after Maxwell filtering the data hold at most about 80 independent components.
- **BatchNorm adaptation (A0).** Allowed now, but its label-free check showed damage on every family. Only a partial blend is worth a file.
- **MEG-XL.** Joining the holdout sentences end to end could give it the \~150 s of context it needs. It still needs an 80 GB GPU, and supervised models win beyond \~2.5 h of labels.
- **A Conformer version of SPW, a band-power member, rank fusion.** As in the 11 Oct report: small or no gains expected.
- **Label-free ensemble weighting** (estimating each member's accuracy from agreement). Skip it: these methods assume members err independently. The per-word members don't, so the weights would hand the vote back to that family, the pattern that lost on 11 Oct.

* **A mid-size G2 (8 layers × 512, with EMA).** The 204M model lost and you declined this; a small gain at best.
* **Distilling the mix into one student on training data.** Distillation tends to lose the diversity the mix relies on, so it is only useful as pick 9's single-model fallback.
* **Vocabulary-only fine-tuning blended back with WiSE-FT.** The one untested version of L's idea; low priority after L's 24-point loss.

## Still off-limits, and already ruled out

**Still off-limits under rule 4**

- Learning labels through the leaderboard, such as submissions built to read off individual rows.
- Looking up the holdout sentences outside the data: matching rows to example sentences in the organisers' papers, or rebuilding the stimulus list.
- Undoing the files' shuffle with the stored shuffle\_seed. If the original order follows the stimulus list, that recovers labels, not signal.
- Other subjects' files, until the organisers confirm them.

**Ruled out by your own board results**

| Tried | Board result |
| --- | --- |
| Re-weighting families or files | −0.54 to −1.22 |
| Six SPW seeds instead of three | −0.54 |
| Time masking (M), a 2.5 s readout (R), a word transformer after the readout (H) | −0.75, −1.04, −1.22 |
| Training on runs made only of the 50 words (L) | 39.83 alone, 24 points below G2 |
| Re-ranking by word-pair frequency in training text | −0.8, −2.3, −3.6 |

| Normalising each window by its own statistics (I1, I2) | 6 to 7 points lost |
| A 204M-parameter model | 49.7, against 58.0 for the 5.85M version |
| Averaging over onset shifts at test time | +0.25, then −1.0 |
| Other subjects' training data in subject 0's model | 54.79 alone, against 63.41 |

L and the word-pair results also argue against fitting a word-order model on the holdout itself. It is allowed now, but it would likely repeat those losses.

## Caveats

- Every gain estimate is my judgement. The literature numbers come from vision benchmarks (TransCLIP, ZLaP, Noisy Student) and will shrink on noisy MEG embeddings.
- The cross-subject simulation is a toy. The real gain depends on how much of subject 0's error all listeners share, which nobody has measured; the local test in change 1 is how to find out.
- The board is noisier than the 11 Oct report assumed. A flipped row on a common word moves the score by a few tenths, but on a word with one public row it moves 2.0 points, as on 7 Oct. A one- to two-point gap between two files can be a single row.
- Transductive and self-training steps can fail without warning. The label-free checks catch damage, not gains, so the board still decides.
- The organisers' answer came by message. Keep its date and wording with the code you hand over.
- Reaching 73 still needs +3.2 on the public board.
